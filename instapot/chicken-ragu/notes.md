# Chicken Ragù — Notes

Reasoning behind the non-obvious choices, and where this can go wrong.

## A sauce and a soup want opposite things from a pressure cooker

This is the central constraint and it drives the whole design.

Soup wants liquid, and the sealed pot's zero evaporation is a feature. **Sauce wants concentration, and zero evaporation is the enemy** — you cannot reduce anything under pressure. Whatever water goes in comes back out.

So: almost no added liquid going in, and a **mandatory open-lid reduction on Sauté at the end**. Pressure buys you depth — roughly a three-hour simmer's flavor in 15 minutes. It cannot buy you thickness. If you skip step 4, you get chicken tomato soup.

Corollary: the cup of liquid the chicken thighs release, which counted in your favor in the tortilla soup, works against you here. It lands directly on the reduction. The rendered fat is a real gain though — it gives richness a vegetarian version has to buy with olive oil.

## Don't use canned diced tomatoes

Diced tomatoes are treated with calcium chloride specifically so they hold their shape. That is the exact opposite of what a sauce needs — they'll still be firm cubes after an hour of cooking. Whole peeled (crushed by hand) or crushed only.

## Grate the vegetables instead of blending

Onion and carrot through a box grater dissolve completely during cooking. There's nothing left to blend, which sidesteps the no-immersion-blender problem entirely and is what a fine soffritto is for in classic ragù. A potato masher handles anything stubborn after cooking.

An immersion blender is worth owning anyway (~$30) — it would also make the tortilla soup tortilla slurry trivial and removes the hot-liquid-in-a-sealed-blender hazard. Not needed for this recipe.

## Cook the mushroom water off

Mushrooms are mostly water. If you move them into the pot while they're still weeping, that water is reduction you've deferred to step 4. Eight to ten minutes in the skillet until they release *and* re-brown is time well spent. It also builds fond for the wine to dissolve.

## Pull the chicken before reducing

Twenty-five minutes of open-lid boiling turns already-cooked shredded thigh stringy and dry. Take it out, reduce the sauce hard, then return it to warm through. Costs nothing and protects the meat.

## Burn error prevention

1. Sauté off-pot, so no scorched fond is welded to the element.
2. Thin liquid on the bottom (1/2 cup water or broth), nothing starchy against it.
3. Stir the dal into that liquid rather than leaving it piled.
4. **Tomatoes on top, unstirred.** Acidic tomato against the element is the classic trigger.

Thick tomato purée is among the most scorch-prone things you can pressure cook. The layering isn't optional here the way it's merely prudent in the soup.

## Dal

Same trick as the tortilla soup, and it works better here: red lentils or moong dal dissolve completely into tomato, add protein and body, and actively *help* the thickening problem instead of fighting it. Invisible in the finished sauce.

Storage: dried split legumes in a sealed dry jar don't spoil — check only for musty smell, moisture clumping, or small dark beetles and webbing.

## Acid balance

Most tomato sauce recipes call for sugar because canned tomatoes are sharp. Here the grated carrots contribute real sweetness and the dal starch rounds the edges, so you'll likely need less or none. **Taste before adding any.** If it's still sharp, 1 Tbsp balsamic is better than sugar — it adds depth rather than just muting.

## The butter finish

Three tablespoons of butter stirred in off heat is a Marcella Hazan move and it disproportionately changes the mouthfeel — the sauce goes from acidic and thin-feeling to round and glossy. Off heat matters; boiled butter just separates. Good olive oil works if you want it dairy-free.

## Fennel seed

The biggest single flavor lever in the recipe. Whole fennel bloomed in oil is what makes tomato sauce taste like it has Italian sausage in it, without the sausage. Bloom it first, before the vegetables — they go in right after and stop it scorching.

## Heat strategy

Same as the tortilla soup: nothing capsaicin-bearing in the pot, all heat on the table. Calabrian chili paste is the best of the three options if you can find it — fruity rather than just hot.

## Capacity

Three 28 oz cans plus 3 lb of chicken overshoots the 8 qt max fill once the grated vegetables are in. Two cans with the full 3 lb of chicken is already a meat-dominant ragù ratio, so nothing is lost. Legumes also foam, which is the other reason not to crowd it — and why the natural release isn't optional.

## Make-ahead

Freezes well, better than the tortilla soup does. Unlike the soup, this won't need thinning on reheat — if anything it tightens further in the fridge, which is fine for a sauce. Loosen with a splash of pasta water at serving time.
